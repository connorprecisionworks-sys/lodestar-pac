01 — The intuition

The trillion-dollar headline is the trap.

The story everyone tells: asteroids hold trillions in platinum and gold, so we fly out, fill a hold, and bring the treasure home. Our own value model will happily quote a stony asteroid at $75.7 trillion.

That number is real arithmetic and a terrible plan. It multiplies tonnes of metal by today's Earth price, and almost every word of that assumption breaks the moment you actually deliver. The rest of this paper is why.

02 — The gravity-well tax

Bringing mass home is the expensive direction.

Every kilogram pays a toll measured in delta-v, the velocity change a rocket must supply. Earth's gravity well is the deepest one nearby: just climbing from the surface to low orbit costs 9.4 km/s, more than the entire in-space leg of a mining trip.

So the cheapest place to keep asteroid material is where it already floats. Hauling it down Earth's well, the one direction the headline assumes, is the one you least want to pay for. In our own engine, chemical propulsion cannot even close the round trip.

03 — The price-collapse paradox

The treasure destroys its own value on arrival.

Platinum is worth roughly $30,000/kg for one reason: scarcity. About 10,000 tonnes have ever been mined in all of human history. A single 1-km metallic asteroid holds an estimated 83,000 tonnes of platinum-group metal, eight times that entire history.

Deliver even a sliver and the price falls toward the cost of refining. The asset is only worth trillions because you cannot get it, and the moment you can, you cannot.

04 — The cost-value gap

The bulk numbers never close.

Strip out the fantasy price and value the rock as what it is: bulk material. Across compositions that is $0.62 to $4.45 per kg on Earth. A returned thousand-tonne haul is worth under $1 million.

Against a round-trip mission costing billions in hardware, propellant, and years, the value-to-cost ratio for Earth return sits far below one. You spend a fortune to deliver a rounding error.

05 — The inversion

The same kilogram, worth a fortune in orbit.

Here is the flip. That bulk material is worth $0.64/kg sold on Earth, but a kilogram already in orbit is worth what it would cost to launch one from the ground: roughly $2,000/kg, even after a decade of falling launch prices.

Same rock, same atoms, a ~3,000× swing, decided entirely by location. You never pay the gravity-well tax twice. You use the material where it is.

06 — What is actually worth mining

Rank by use in space, not price on Earth.

Water is the keystone. Split it and you have hydrogen and oxygen, the most common rocket propellant, made into a gas station in orbit. The same water is radiation shielding, drinking water, and the basis of life support and agriculture for any colony.

Next come structural metals to build habitats and shields in place. Platinum-group metals are useful only in trace amounts, as in-space catalysts, never as an export. The ranking is the exact inverse of the Earth-price story.

07 — What if we're wrong

The honest edges of this argument.

A framework is only worth trusting if it names where it could fail. Four places this one might.

In-space processing is immature

Mining, melting, and electrolyzing an asteroid in microgravity has never been done at scale. The value only exists if the processing works, and that engineering is the real frontier, not the rock.

A niche Earth export might survive

A small, genuinely scarce product (specific isotopes, ultra-pure samples for research) could justify return in tiny quantities. That is a curiosity business, not the trillion-dollar one.

Cheap launch cuts both ways

If launch costs crater, the in-space premium shrinks too. But propellant is mostly mass, and mass is exactly what you most want to avoid launching, so water-in-orbit keeps its edge longest.

The timeline is decades

None of this is a this-decade business. It is the architecture of a cislunar economy that has to be bootstrapped one depot at a time. We are arguing about direction, not a quarterly return.

The business case is never having to ship it up.

The fortune in asteroids was never a hold full of platinum falling to Earth. It is a fuel depot in orbit, a reservoir for a colony, and structure you did not have to launch. The prize is the cheap, abundant, boring material, used exactly where the gravity well is already behind you.
